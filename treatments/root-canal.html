Root canal treatment, without the dread.

Thirty of our Google reviews are about root canals. The word that keeps appearing in them is not “painful” — it is the opposite. Here is exactly what the appointment involves.

Book this treatment Ask a question

The reputation is worse than the reality. A root canal is uncomfortable because of the infection that made it necessary — not because of the treatment. Removing the infected tissue is what stops the pain.

What a root canal actually does

Inside every tooth is a chamber of soft tissue — the pulp — containing nerves and blood vessels. When decay or a crack lets bacteria in, that tissue becomes inflamed and then infected. Because the tooth is sealed, the pressure has nowhere to go. That is the throbbing pain, and the abscess at the root tip.

A root canal removes the infected pulp, cleans and shapes the canals, disinfects them, and seals them. The tooth stays in your mouth, keeps its root, and keeps the bone around it. It stops being a living tooth; it does not stop being a useful one.

Signs you might need one

  • Pain that lingers for more than a few seconds after something hot or cold.
  • A dull ache when you bite down, or tenderness to tapping.
  • Swelling of the gum near a tooth, or a small pimple-like bump that comes and goes.
  • Discolouration of a single tooth.
  • Sometimes, no symptoms at all — found on an X-ray during a routine check-up.

Pain on its own is not a diagnosis. A tooth that is sensitive for a second and settles is often just recession or a lost filling. The examination and radiograph are what decide it.

During the visit

  1. Isolation. The tooth is numbed, then isolated with a rubber dam — a sheet that keeps saliva and bacteria out of the canals while we work. It is not optional; a root canal done without one is a gamble.
  2. Access. A small opening is made through the top of the tooth.
  3. Cleaning and shaping. The canals are measured, then cleaned with rotary nickel-titanium files and irrigated with disinfecting solutions. This is the part that takes the time.
  4. Sealing. The canals are dried and filled with a rubber-like material, and the access is closed.
  5. The crown. A root-treated tooth becomes brittle. Almost always it needs a crown to survive biting forces — and that is a separate, later appointment.

Afterwards

For two or three days the tooth may feel tender when you bite, because the ligament around the root has been irritated. That settles. A painkiller is usually enough.

Until the crown is on, avoid chewing hard food on that side — an unrestored root-treated tooth can split, and a split tooth cannot be saved. This is the single most common way a good root canal ends badly.

If swelling returns, or the tooth becomes painful again weeks later, tell us. Retreatment is sometimes necessary and is far easier than leaving it.

What drives the cost

The number of canals is the main factor — a front tooth typically has one, a molar has three or four — along with whether it is a first treatment or a retreatment of a tooth someone else started, and what kind of crown goes on afterwards.

We quote after the examination, with the crown priced separately so you can see what you are paying for. If a tooth is not restorable, we will say that too, rather than charging for a root canal that will fail.

Questions

Things people ask before booking.

Is a root canal painful?

The treatment is done under local anaesthesia, so it should not be. Most of the pain people associate with root canals comes from the infection beforehand — which is exactly what the treatment resolves.

How many visits will it take?

One or two for most teeth. Molars with curved or calcified canals, retreatment, or a tooth with a large abscess may need an extra visit with medication in between.

Can I just have the tooth removed instead?

You can, and sometimes that is the right call — if the tooth is split or there is too little left to restore. But extraction is cheaper today and usually costs more later, once the gap needs an implant or bridge. We will explain both.

Do I really need a crown afterwards?

For back teeth, essentially yes. The tooth is more brittle after treatment and the chewing loads are high. Front teeth sometimes manage with a filling, depending on how much tooth is left.

How long does a root canal last?

With a good seal, a proper crown and reasonable hygiene, many last decades. The usual reasons for failure are a leaked restoration, a cracked tooth, or untreated canals — all of which is why the crown matters as much as the root canal itself.
